### R00b-F01 Prioritize the 90% of the screen
- principle: The environment is roughly 90% of the screen, and there is always more to build than time allows, so environment work is ranked by what matters most to the overall game experience, decided with the game designer and concept artist, not by individual rock or tree.
- why: Without the environment the player sees "an empty void with some characters and UI"; it carries the world, but polishing every piece equally burns the budget on things no player looks at.
- apply: Before building a stage, the environment designer writes a ranked list with the game designer: play spaces and routes first, landmarks and first view next, filler last. Budget build effort and triangles to match that rank. [inference] A default split: top-ranked areas get hero kit pieces and unique assets; background gets reused kit pieces.
- check: At the game camera, capture the 3-5 most-visited views; every top-ranked item must be visible and finished in them, and low-ranked items may stay simple. Count unique assets per area against the ranked list.
- mistake: Treating every tree and rock as equally important ("This is so much work! How do you do all this?").
- example: "It's basically 90% of the screen"; artists rank with designers and concept artists.
- roblox_fit: Same on Roblox; phones have a low draw budget, so ranking also decides where parts and triangles go (meshes max 20,000 triangles each: knowledge/external/roblox-creator-docs/art/modeling/specifications.md:34).

### R00b-F02 Environment process order: blockout first, lighting and polish last
- principle: Concept sets theme and tone; the game designer makes a grey-block blockout (obstacles, where the player goes, where enemies come from); environment art roughs out art that serves both the concept and the blockout; then models, textures and colour are refined while the game is playtested a lot; only when it is playtested and working do lighting, post-processing and polish go in.
- why: Gameplay constraints are fixed before art is spent on them; lighting and polish depend on final geometry, so doing them early wastes work.
- apply: Stage jobs run: art bible mood image (reference-images) → level-design greybox in Studio with plain Parts → environment rough kit over the greybox → refine + playtest captures → Lighting, Atmosphere, post effects and polish last. Do not set final Lighting before the route is playtested.
- check: The job record shows a playtested greybox before any final art; the lighting pass is dated after the last route change. A Studio capture of the greybox exists at the game camera.
- mistake: Polishing art and light before the layout is proven (implied by "at the end, when it's all been playtested, and it's working, we'll add lighting").
- example: The process breakdown: concept → grey blocks → rough art → refine + playtest → lighting, post, polish.
- roblox_fit: Roblox docs recommend the same greybox step and placeholder packages that update to detailed versions later (knowledge/external/roblox-creator-docs/tutorials/curriculums/environmental-art/greybox-your-environment.md:17; projects/assets/packages.md:19).

### R00b-F03 Push the design with paint-overs
- principle: Turning grey boxes into a look takes constant back-and-forth with concept: paint-overs and jamming on ideas to push the design.
- why: The concept captures "the essence of how the environment should really look and feel"; paint-overs on the real blockout show where the build drifts from it.
- apply: Capture the greybox or rough build at the game camera and ask the reference-images skill for a paint-over of that exact capture in the art bible style; build toward the paint-over. Repeat after each major pass.
- check: Side-by-side of current capture and its paint-over; list the differences in shape, value and light and confirm each was closed or consciously rejected.
- mistake: not stated (implied: building from the concept once and never comparing again).
- example: "Constant paintovers, jamming on ideas to push the design."
- roblox_fit: No change; captures come from Studio MCP screen_capture at the game camera.
- roles: environment-designer, art-director, reference-images, modeler

### R00b-F04 One constraint, many fictional answers
- principle: A gameplay constraint can be dressed in many ways; a boundary can be a wall, a building, or a cliff overlooking the ocean. Pick the solution that makes the game most beautiful and believable.
- why: All options serve the same gameplay, so the choice is free to serve mood, story and beauty; an obviously artificial wall breaks belief.
- apply: For each level-design constraint (boundary, choke point, cover, height gate), the environment designer lists 2-3 fictional solutions and picks with the art director by fit with the setting. Record the choice in the stage notes.
- check: At the game camera, no boundary reads as an arbitrary invisible or plain wall; ask "why is this here in the world?" for each and answer it in one line.
- mistake: Solving a constraint with the first generic blocker.
- example: Gameplay boundary as wall, building, or cliff over the ocean.
- roblox_fit: Invisible walls are common on Roblox; prefer a believable visual blocker plus a collision part matched to it.
- roles: environment-designer, level-design, game-designer, art-director
- topic: world-level-readability (secondary: style-identity)

### R00b-F05 Every environment piece says "what I am" and "what you can do"
- principle: An environment must communicate a believable setting and the gameplay-critical information at the same time.
- why: Players read both together; a cliff that is clearly a cliff and clearly climbable lets them act without a tutorial.
- apply: For each interactive environment element, write its two lines: setting ("I'm obviously a rocky cliff") and gameplay ("very clear ledge climbing gameplay"). Make the gameplay part read with shape, value or colour that is used consistently (same ledge treatment everywhere; shootable objects share one look).
- check: Show a capture at the game camera to a fresh reviewer agent with no notes: it must name both what the place is and what can be done there (climb, shoot, rest). Grayscale check: gameplay surfaces must still stand out.
- mistake: Places that look nice but hide what is interactive, or are readable but look like a test level.
- example: The cliff with ledges, the eerie hallway with a sack of goo to shoot, the pool of water that may or may not be safe.
- roblox_fit: Phone screens make small cues hard to see; gameplay cues must read at small size, so test at thumbnail size with tools/art/look_check.py.
- roles: environment-designer, level-design, game-designer, modeler, vfx-designer, qa-lead

### R00b-F06 Environments are shapes that lead the eye
- principle: At its core an environment is a collection of shapes that lead the eye, like any image; design and lay out those shapes to support a clear hierarchy of focus and guide the player.
- why: The eye follows shapes and lines; a clear hierarchy keeps the game clear.
- apply: In the greybox and kit layout, point big shapes (paths, walls, cliff lines, roof lines) toward the goal; keep one main focal shape per view. Recap tools: composition, lighting and careful detail.
- check: Black-fill or grayscale capture at the game camera: trace the main lines; they should converge on the goal or next route. Blur test (heavy downscale): the goal area must still be the main read.
- mistake: Shapes that point everywhere or away from where the player must go.
- example: "An environment is a collection of shapes that lead the eye, just like any image."
- roblox_fit: Third-person and top-down cameras change which lines are seen; check from the actual game camera, not a free camera.
- roles: environment-designer, level-design, art-director, film-director
- topic: visual-hierarchy (secondary: composition-focus)
- lines: R00b:1863-1885, R00b:2117-2119

### R00b-F07 Establishing composition at the start of a level
- principle: Start a level with an establishing composition that tells the player where the hero is and what they are about to do.
- why: Orientation first: the player understands the space and the goal before acting.
- apply: The first view after spawn (or after a teleport) frames the goal or main landmark; the level designer sets the spawn position and facing so this view is what the camera shows. A cutscene is optional; the spawn camera itself can do it.
- check: Capture the very first frame after spawn at the default camera: the goal or landmark is visible and is the strongest focal point.
- mistake: Spawning players facing a wall or with no view of where to go.
- example: Ratchet & Clank levels began with an establishing composition.
- roblox_fit: Set spawn orientation (SpawnLocation facing) and default camera; on phones the first view must read at small size.
- roles: level-design, environment-designer, film-director, game-designer

### R00b-F08 Light and shadow make focal points
- principle: Light and shadow add beauty and believability and also create focal points; spotlighting and shadows create contrast that becomes a key compositional element to lead the player.
- why: The eye goes to the brightest, highest-contrast area first.
- apply: Put the strongest local light and the highest value contrast at the goal, door, pickup or path; keep surrounding areas lower. Use SpotLight/PointLight with shadows on for key focal points, Atmosphere and lower ambient elsewhere.
- check: Grayscale capture at the game camera: the brightest area must be the intended focus. Measure with tools/art/look_check.py value check; the focus region should hold the top value contrast.
- mistake: Even, flat light everywhere so nothing leads the eye.
- example: Spotlights and shadows used as compositional elements.
- roblox_fit: Local lights (PointLight, SpotLight, SurfaceLight) have Color, Brightness and Shadows (knowledge/external/roblox-creator-docs/effects/light-sources.md:29). Many shadow-casting lights cost performance on phones [unverified]; use few, placed at focal points.
- roles: environment-designer, art-director, level-design, film-director, vfx-designer
- topic: light-colour (secondary: composition-focus, value-contrast)
- lines: R00b:1901-1921

### R00b-F09 Materials plus light set the mood
- principle: The materials of a space and how the lighting interacts with them are key to the mood of the environment.
- why: Rough, wet, metal or soft surfaces react to light differently, and that reaction carries the feeling.
- apply: Choose materials and lighting together in the art bible (for example wet stone with low cold light). In Studio, test each key material under the stage's final Lighting, not under default light.
- check: Capture key materials under the final lighting at the game camera; compare to the mood anchor image in the art bible.
- mistake: Picking materials under default light and lighting later.
- example: "The materials of the space, and how the lighting interacts with those materials".
- roblox_fit: Material response depends on Roblox materials/SurfaceAppearance and Lighting technology; preview at the target quality level [unverified].
- roles: environment-designer, modeler, art-director
- topic: materials-detail (secondary: light-colour)

### R00b-F10 A bright focal light can hide a surprise
- principle: A bright light as focal point can distract the player from something you want to surprise them with.
- why: Attention goes to the light, so the area outside it is unwatched.
- apply: For a scare, ambush or hidden reward, put a bright focal light on one side and stage the surprise in the darker, low-contrast side or behind the player's line of sight.
- check: Grayscale capture: the surprise element must be in a low-contrast area while the light is the top focus; confirm in a playtest that testers do not spot it early.
- mistake: Surprises placed in the most lit or most contrasted part of the screen.
- example: "Hello..." gag: a creature appears while the eye is on the light.
- roblox_fit: Works the same; on phones peripheral detail is even less noticed.
- roles: level-design, environment-designer, game-designer, film-director
- topic: composition-focus (secondary: light-colour)

### R00b-F11 Rest areas versus detail areas
- principle: Careful application of detail: shifting between large areas of rest and areas with more detail guides where the player looks.
- why: Detail attracts the eye; areas of rest give contrast so detail can work.
- apply: Concentrate texture and prop detail at goals, routes and landmarks; keep large plain areas (simple materials, few props) between them. Same idea for UI: dense information only where the decision is.
- check: Downscale a capture to thumbnail size: detail clusters should sit at focal points with clear quiet areas between; count props per zone and compare to the focus map.
- mistake: Even detail density everywhere, making the whole screen noisy (see F16: the "busy and noisy" forest).
- example: "Shifting between large areas of rest, and areas with more detail".
- roblox_fit: Fewer props in rest areas also saves draw budget on phones.
- roles: environment-designer, modeler, art-director, ui-ux-designer, level-design, reviewer

### R00b-F12 Environmental storytelling: someone lives here
- principle: Everything from the overall layout to single props can tell a story; climate, age and culture show through the environment and reinforce the game's fantasy. Signs of daily life make a level feel inhabited, not dead.
- why: The player infers history and inhabitants from what is left behind, which deepens the fantasy without text.
- apply: For each stage, write 3 story facts (climate, age, who lives here and what happened) and assign each to visible props and layout (food remnants, repairs, laundry, ruins). Prompt reference images with these facts.
- check: A reviewer agent viewing captures without notes should state at least one of the story facts. Count story props per main area (at least one cluster per area [inference]).
- mistake: "a dead level" with nobody living there.
- example: Horizon Zero Dawn's collapsed robots imply a forgotten war; Kingdom Come: Deliverance camps with remnants of food, boarded-over potholes, bandits' laundry.
- roblox_fit: Story props must be big enough to read at the game camera on a phone; small detail is lost.
- roles: environment-designer, modeler, art-director, level-design, game-designer
- topic: world-level-readability (secondary: style-identity)

### R00b-F13 Research real places for unique details
- principle: Environment artists research all the time: observe real environments, how they are laid out, and their unique details.
- why: Real places supply specific, believable details that invention misses.
- apply: For each new setting, gather real-world reference (layout, odd details: pipes, dark corners, grates, old kitchens) and note the specific details to use; feed them into reference-image prompts and the kit list.
- check: The stage's reference board lists real sources and the details taken from them; each detail appears in the build or is marked rejected.
- mistake: Generic, invented details.
- example: Vacation photos of "weird pipes, dark weird corners, dirty grates, and old kitchens".
- roblox_fit: No change.

### R00b-F14 Scale: block out with a character in the scene
- principle: Scale is easy to mess up and ruins believability. Good scale cues are things the character interacts with a lot: chairs, doorknobs, railings, stairs. Always block out with a character in for scale reference.
- why: Players judge size against their own avatar and familiar objects.
- apply: Put a standard player character in every greybox and every model review scene (Blender and Studio). Size interaction props (doors, stairs, railings, seats) to the character first, then the rest.
- check: Capture the character next to each interaction prop at the game camera; measure heights in studs against the character. Flag any furniture or door off by more than the style allows.
- mistake: The artist's own: "blocked out a whole room without putting a character in for scale reference, and ended up having him sit at, like, a twenty-foot desk."
- example: The twenty-foot desk.
- roblox_fit: Roblox docs' greybox example keeps doorways and hallways at least 10 studs wide and walls at least 10 studs tall for two players, default jump height 5 studs and camera room (knowledge/external/roblox-creator-docs/tutorials/curriculums/environmental-art/greybox-your-environment.md:77). Roblox often exaggerates doorways for the third-person camera.
- roles: environment-designer, level-design, modeler, qa-lead, reviewer

### R00b-F15 Modular kits: variance without visible duplicates
- principle: Large worlds must run smoothly and cannot be custom built piece by piece; keep polycount low, reuse models and textures, and build reusable modular chunks ("elaborate Legos") with enough variance for very different looks but generic enough not to be recognized as duplicates. Rotating or manipulating one piece makes it look new.
- why: Reuse saves memory, draw cost and build time; visible repeats break believability.
- apply: Modeler and environment designer plan a kit per stage: walls, floors, trims, props, rocks. Design each piece to read differently when rotated (a crate with different faces; a rock usable on any side). Share one trim/texture set across kit pieces. Place with random rotation and scale within limits.
- check: Count unique meshes and textures per stage against the budget; in captures at the game camera, look for the same piece visible twice in one view at the same angle and fix it.
- mistake: Custom-building every piece, or reusing obviously identical pieces side by side.
- example: A crate turned around to look like a different crate; one rock manipulated many ways.
- roblox_fit: Packages let one kit piece be reused and mass-updated (knowledge/external/roblox-creator-docs/projects/assets/packages.md:3, :14); a shared SurfaceAppearance trim-sheet package across modular assets (tutorials/curriculums/environmental-art/assemble-an-asset-library.md:93). Mesh limit 20,000 triangles each (art/modeling/specifications.md:34).
- roles: environment-designer, modeler, level-design, architect, art-director

### R00b-F16 Solve the problem behind the note, not the proposed solution
- principle: When someone gives a note or asks for a tool, find the problem they are trying to solve before acting; the solution they suggest is often not the best one. Ask "What are you trying to do?"
- why: The note-giver sees the symptom; the maker knows the craft and can find a cheaper or better fix.
- apply: For every review note or brief that names a solution, the receiving agent restates the underlying problem in one line and, if it differs, proposes its fix and says why. Reviewers should write notes as problems ("busy and noisy"), not as solutions.
- check: Each fix in a hand-back names the problem it solves; after the fix, recheck the original problem (for example noise level in a capture), not just whether the suggested change was made.
- mistake: Doing what the note says literally, such as deleting all the trees.
