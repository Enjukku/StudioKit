## Findings

### R11-F01 Four-stage concept pipeline, explore wide
- principle: Character design runs research → exploration (as many ideas as possible) → iteration on the strongest one or two → finish to what the next maker needs. Exploration produces far more than survives: he says a real exploration is "about five times the amount of art" of the one-hour demo.
- why: Breadth first means the chosen direction won against real alternatives; iteration then refines one idea instead of polishing a guess.
- apply: In reference-images, plan rounds: (1) a research moodboard, (2) an exploration sheet of many distinct options (sound default [inference]: 6-12 thumbnails across clearly different directions), (3) iteration variants of the 1-2 picks, (4) one final sheet for the modeler. Do not render final quality in round 2. Source timings for scale: research about a week, concepting 2-3 months, exploration "a few weeks".
- check: Count distinct directions in the exploration round (silhouettes that differ in black fill, not just colour swaps); at least one option per round should be a big departure from the base.
- mistake: Jumping from one idea straight to finish.
- example: Katarina: base rendition, then darker variants A/B/C, a vampire-hunter last bid, then an A+B merge brought to concept finish.
- roblox_fit: Same for any Roblox character, pet, weapon or building; the image model makes breadth cheap, so the risk is too little selection, not too little output [inference].

### R11-F02 Research: what it has, what it lacks, one unexpected element
- principle: Start with what is expected (the obvious wins, e.g. an assassin stays light and agile), then look for the unexpected element. List what the subject already has and what it lacks, and keep its faction look so it still belongs. Fashion is "a very underutilized element of character design".
- why: Expected traits keep it recognisable; one unexpected element makes it feel evolved instead of copied.
- apply: Moodboard prompt sections: (a) the subject's current material reads (here: black leathers, spiked metal bracer, red hair), (b) faction/world style to keep, (c) real-world fashion references outside games. Write in the brief "keep:" and "try:" lists.
- check: The moodboard has at least one source family that is not from games (fashion, other disciplines); the brief names the one unexpected element in one line.
- mistake: Moodboards made only of other game art, which repeats the genre.
- example: Katarina's research was mostly fashion research while keeping her a "citizen of Noxus".
- roblox_fit: Same; for Roblox the faction look is the game's art bible anchors.

### R11-F03 Take the big swings early
- principle: The concept stage is the only place for large changes, so try them first: "if we can change it we should, just to see". If the final stays close to the base, that must be because the tested alternatives lost.
- why: Later crafts cannot make big swings; untried directions are lost for good.
- apply: Each exploration round includes at least one radical option (his first test on long-haired characters: cut the hair short). Treat everything as "up for grabs ... until proven otherwise", then decide what is core at the end.
- check: The exploration sheet shows at least one option that changes a primary trait (hair shape, silhouette, main material); the hand-back says why the chosen one won.
- mistake: Doing "just what came before".
- example: Short bob on Katarina; full mask on a character who never hides her face.
- roblox_fit: Same.
- roles: art-director, reference-images, modeler, game-designer, vfx-designer, ui-ux-designer

### R11-F04 Know the character to decide on their behalf
- principle: Approach a character holistically: who they are in canon, what tragedies happened and how they react, and who they might want to be next. That knowledge lets you make design decisions on their behalf.
- why: Costume, pose and detail choices become consistent when they follow one personality.
- apply: Before images, write a short character card (temperament, history, how they move, what they would never wear); attach it to every reference-images prompt and to the animator's and sound designer's brief.
- check: Each design choice in the hand-back can be traced to a line of the card.
- mistake: Treating early exploration as only quick and careless; he takes time on details "that help me understand who they are".
- example: Darker Katarina as a meticulous, obsessive assassin who lost her sense of self.
- roblox_fit: Same.
- roles: art-director, reference-images, modeler, animator, sound-designer, film-director, game-designer

### R11-F05 Spend production cost only where it shows
- principle: Some features are very expensive downstream: lots of cloth, bulky capes, tassels, long hair. Use them only when the cost is worth it: it makes a better, more aspirational design and the character is not also expensive elsewhere.
- why: "Games are expensive"; months of a co-worker's time can go into something that barely changes the look.
- apply: In the design hand-back, list each costly element (cape, loose cloth, long hair, many dangling parts) with the reason it earns its cost. In our pipeline each such part means extra bones, weights and keyed secondary motion in Blender; default to rigid or bundled shapes [inference].
- check: Count dangling/flowing parts on the final sheet; each one has a written reason; the rigger confirms the count is in budget.
- mistake: Designing for the concept artist's own love of fabric without considering who must make it real.
- example: After Darius the team was "convinced that we'd never do another cape again"; Yasuo's cape and cowl needed delicate animation work.
- roblox_fit: Roblox has no cloth simulation for characters in our pipeline; flowing parts must be keyed bones or layered clothing (WrapLayer cages: knowledge/external/roblox-creator-docs/art/accessories/layered-clothing.md:136). Every extra mesh part also counts against the character triangle budget (art/characters/specifications.md:378).
- roles: art-director, modeler, character-rigger, animator, reference-images, orchestrator, architect

### R11-F06 Lines + shadows + colours = noise, and motion multiplies it
- principle: Line work and shadows are needed to show form, but together, with too many colours, they make "a visual noise generating machine". A few extra details can lose a character completely, and a design that looks simple on the sheet can be a noise nightmare in motion with shifting light and effects.
- why: Every edge, shadow and colour change is a signal; in motion they compete and the form is lost.
- apply: Keep a small number of colour blocks and details per character; decide which forms need outline or strong shadow and leave the rest flat. In Roblox, the noise sources are textures, many small parts, Highlight outlines, strong shadows and effects on top.
- check: Capture the character in motion at game camera with lighting and a typical effect on; grayscale it; if the body parts cannot be told apart, remove detail. look_check.py value and silhouette measures on that capture.
- mistake: "Full send every detail" because it looks "not that complicated on the concept sheet".
- example: Simple 2XKO designs that became noisy once moving, lit and with effects.
- roblox_fit: Many players and effects on screen make noise worse; Highlight is capped at 255 simultaneous instances on the client (knowledge/external/roblox-creator-docs/_api-reference/classes/Highlight.yaml:49-52), so outlines are not a universal fix.

### R11-F07 Nothing in front of the shoulders
- principle: Avoid elements that hang in front of the shoulders and body (capes, cowls). Pauldrons are fine; hanging pieces are not.
- why: Hanging pieces cover the limbs the player tracks and need delicate animation fixes.
- apply: Modeler: keep the shoulder line and arm silhouette clear; mount shoulder pieces on the shoulder, not draped over the chest or arm.
- check: Black-fill silhouette in key poses (attack, idle, run): the arm's outline must stay separate from the torso.
- mistake: Cowls and capes draping forward (Yasuo's case).
- example: Yasuo's cape and cowl were "problematic for the longest time".
- roblox_fit: Same; at phone size an arm hidden by a drape is lost entirely [inference].
- roles: modeler, character-rigger, animator, art-director, reference-images

### R11-F08 Trackable extremities and separable body parts
- principle: Players must be able to track hands, head and feet and tell the head from the torso, arms from the body, legs and feet from each other. Use tone breaks to separate parts on a dark costume.
- why: Action reads come from extremities; separable parts make every move legible.
- apply: Plan a value break at each joint area that matters (neck, wrists, ankles): skin vs dark cloth, light metal vs dark leather. Hands and feet get the strongest contrast.
- check: Grayscale capture at game camera: each extremity has a visible value edge against the adjacent part; silhouette check at thumbnail size still shows separate limbs.
- mistake: An all-dark costume where limbs merge.
- example: Katarina is mostly black leather; her skin tone "ends up being a way to save that element".
- roblox_fit: Small phone screens and third-person cameras make extremity contrast more important [inference].
- roles: art-director, modeler, animator, character-rigger, vfx-designer, reviewer, reference-images

### R11-F09 Low mental load on your own character
- principle: While tracking the opponent, the player must not also have to work out what their own character is doing. Everything must be recognisable; animators turn the designed elements into strong key poses.
- why: Attention goes to the threat; an unclear own character breaks the input-feedback loop.
- apply: Animator: each attack's key pose shows the designed hook (weapon, gauntlet) clearly; game-designer: own-character feedback is readable without looking at it directly.
- check: Frame-by-frame capture: at the key pose of each move, the move is identifiable from the silhouette alone.
- mistake: not stated.
- example: Animators take designed elements "to create really strong key poses".
- roblox_fit: In a third-person Roblox camera the player sees their own back, so the readable cues must sit on the back and outline [inference].
- roles: game-designer, animator, art-director, vfx-designer, ui-ux-designer, qa-lead

### R11-F10 Design is a functional vehicle, not a nice picture
- principle: "Design is not about making good art, it's about making these functional vehicles for other things." Judge a design by what it adds to or takes from player experience and how easy it is to build. Be flashy, but never a game people "need to fight in order to understand".
- why: Clarity is what lets players learn and play at a high level; concept sets the scaffolding for every other role.
- apply: Every design hand-back answers: what does this add to or detract from play clarity, and what does it cost to build? Reviewers reject "looks cool" as the only reason.
- check: Review form has two required lines: clarity effect, build cost.
- mistake: Treating a concept task as only "a nice looking character that is just meant to be cool"; assuming illustration skill equals design skill.
- example: His teachers: being good at art "is not good enough for what design demands of you".
- roblox_fit: Same.
- roles: art-director, reviewer, orchestrator, game-designer, modeler, ui-ux-designer, vfx-designer, marketing

### R11-F11 Find the genre's demands first
- principle: Do not assume you know how to design for a MOBA, a fighting game, an MMO or a beat-'em-up; each genre and gameplay has its own demands, and design is "the best solution given very particular sets of parameters". Some genre conventions exist for a reason (they let new players in) and cannot be dropped.
- why: Priorities differ by genre; a design good for one camera and play style can fail in another.
- apply: Before a craft brief, list the game's parameters (camera, screen size, player count, session pace) from the game profile; the design is judged against them.
- check: The brief states the parameters; the review cites them.
- mistake: Confidence "purely due to lack of challenge".
- example: Fighting-game clarity conventions kept even though 2XKO wants to look new.
- roblox_fit: Roblox parameters: phones, touch, third-person or top-down camera, many players, engine budgets.
- roles: game-designer, art-director, orchestrator, architect, ui-ux-designer, level-design, environment-designer

### R11-F12 Design for the side the camera shows
- principle: The camera changes how a character is perceived. League's top-down view lets design focus on the top half; 2XKO's flat side view with P1 on the left facing right shows mostly the right side. Move core identity details to the side that is seen. "We're a fighting game first and a League of Legends game second."
- why: A defining detail on the hidden side is lost in a fast game; each important detail needs "a moment to shine".
- apply: Read the game camera from the game profile, then place identity details on the faces the camera sees most (for side view: one side; for top-down: head, shoulders, top of weapons). Accept mirrored or moved details versus the source design.
- check: Render from the game camera at its usual angles; list identity details and confirm each is visible in most frames.
- mistake: Keeping details where the original design had them when the camera never shows that side.
- example: Katarina's left-eye scar would be lost in side view; Vi's details were flipped and players asked whether she was canon.
- roblox_fit: Third-person Roblox shows mostly the back and top of the player's own avatar; top-down shows head and shoulders [inference]. Put identity on back, shoulders and head.
- roles: art-director, modeler, reference-images, character-rigger, animator, marketing, environment-designer

### R11-F13 Flip the image to see it fresh
- principle: Mirror the image often; the brain treats the flipped image as new and catches errors: symmetry tilt, one side of the face higher, values, proportion.
- why: The eye adapts to errors in an image it has looked at too long; a sudden change resets it.
- apply: In every self-review of an image or render, also view a horizontally mirrored copy (a one-line image flip in Python) and a grayscale copy.
- check: Compare the original and mirror: eye lines, shoulder lines and verticals stay level; no part looks lopsided only in the mirror.
- mistake: Long looks at the same image; his own flaw: poor symmetry that makes things slant.
- example: He flips constantly and recommends it; rotating the canvas works too but is slower.
- roblox_fit: Same for UI mocks and thumbnails.
- roles: art-director, reviewer, modeler, reference-images, ui-ux-designer, marketing, qa-lead
- topic: critique-feedback (secondary: value-contrast)

### R11-F14 Silhouette is what players see in motion
- principle: About 99% of the time a fighter is moving, jumping, flipping; players do not see most details. They see the silhouette at key poses and when the game lingers (hit-pause) on a strong hit.
- why: Only held moments are internalised; detail outside them is invisible.
- apply: Designers add "a little bit of extra silhouette, dynamic shape" in places that show at key poses; animators hold key poses and hit-pauses where those shapes face the camera; VFX does not cover them at the held frame.
- check: Pull the key-pose and hit-pause frames from the clip (clip_check.py or frame captures); black-fill them; the character type and the hook shape read at thumbnail size.
- mistake: Investing in fine detail that is never seen.
- example: "If we ever ... linger on a moment to make a certain hit feel stronger, those are the things that you really see".
- roblox_fit: Phone screens and busy servers shrink characters further; silhouette matters more [inference].
- roles: art-director, modeler, animator, vfx-designer, character-rigger, reviewer, reference-images
- topic: shape-silhouette (secondary: weight-timing, vfx-reads-timing)

### R11-F15 Chunk the details that matter, cut the rest
- principle: Make important details big enough to read on screen (thin spikes → chunky spikes) and remove or reduce small things that do not improve the silhouette (rivets, buttons, belt buckles). Pull edges out, sharper, more dramatic.
- why: Chunky shapes survive motion and let the player track a limb; removing small noise organises "visual importance".
- apply: Modeler: scale up hook details to silhouette-level size; drop rivets/buttons or put them in texture only at low contrast. Same rule for props, UI icons and VFX shapes.
- check: Silhouette at game camera and at thumbnail size (thumb_check.py): each kept detail changes the outline; any detail invisible at that size is a removal candidate.
- mistake: Form-fitting plates with thin spikes that vanish on screen.
- example: Katarina's gauntlet spikes chunked up so the "dramatic metal shape" lets you track her arm.
- roblox_fit: Small details also cost triangles; cutting them helps the budget (art/characters/specifications.md:378).
- roles: modeler, art-director, reference-images, environment-designer, ui-ux-designer, vfx-designer, reviewer
- topic: shape-silhouette (secondary: visual-hierarchy, materials-detail)

### R11-F16 Larger than life
- principle: Fighting-game characters are larger than life: "double the size, they have double the muscle mass, they have double the personality", leaping out of the screen.
- why: Exaggeration reads at speed and distance and sells the fantasy.
- apply: Push proportions, hook shapes and pose energy past realistic in reference images and models; animators and sound match the push.
- check: Side by side with a realistic proportion figure, the design is clearly exaggerated in the hook areas.
- mistake: not stated.
- example: The 2XKO cast "have all gotten into protein".
- roblox_fit: Same; stylised proportions also read better at small sizes [inference].
- roles: art-director, modeler, animator, reference-images, marketing, vfx-designer, sound-designer

### R11-F17 Explore on varied poses and body types
- principle: The classic concept pose (three-quarter, static, stiff) shows details but robs the character of personality. Try many poses and body types; pose and costume together tell a stronger story, and some clothes do not fit some personalities.
- why: A costume in motion and attitude shows whether it fits the character.
- apply: Reference-images exploration prompts ask for each costume in 2-3 attitude poses (e.g. hunched aggressive, solemn) plus the clean turnaround only at the end for the modeler.
- check: The exploration sheet shows each costume in at least two attitudes; the chosen one reads its temperament from pose.
- mistake: Only stiff three-quarter turnarounds during exploration.
- example: Katarina tried "more hunched, more aggressive" versus "more solemn ... depressed" and dressed differently for each.
- roblox_fit: Same.
- roles: art-director, reference-images, modeler, animator, marketing
- topic: character-design (secondary: references-ideation)

### R11-F18 Proportions read relative to the cast
- principle: Body type tells story and is judged against the roster: in a cast that has become beefier, a character reads thinner even with more muscle than expected.
- why: Players compare characters on the same screen.

### R11-F19 Hold identity anchors, change the rest
- principle: Keep some elements fixed while others change (change the costume, keep the hair). Find the consensus of what makes the character them (red hair, scar). Costume is an accessory: people change clothes and are still recognised by face and mannerisms.
- why: Recognition survives large changes when the anchors stay.
- apply: In each brief list the identity anchors that must not change; variants change only non-anchors. For skins, events and live-ops variants, the anchor list is the test.
- check: Each variant keeps all anchors visible at game camera.
- mistake: Changing everything at once so the character is lost.
- example: Red hair stays red, scar stays, costume is open.
- roblox_fit: Same for skins and cosmetics.

### R11-F20 Material and condition tell the story
- principle: Changing material reads and how custom-made the costume is tells who the character has become: fewer custom-fitted metal pieces and scrounged cotton instead of polished leather show a character who lost herself.
- why: Players read history from wear, fit and material.
- apply: Brief materials with a story reason (polished/custom vs scrounged/worn); in Blender/Roblox express it through material choice and SurfaceAppearance roughness and wear.
- check: Grayscale and colour captures show the material contrast reads at game camera, not only in close-ups.
- mistake: not stated.
- example: Darker Katarina without the leathers, more dishevelled and dystopian.

### R11-F21 One-piece variants, label, merge the best two
- principle: Explore by changing one ensemble piece or trait at a time (jacket, pants, hairstyle, makeup), including trends that may not fit ("big pants"); label options A/B/C; merge the strongest two into the iteration; use a last bid to test an extreme.
- why: Single-change variants show what each element does; merging keeps the best of both.
- apply: Reference-images prompts vary one named element per option; options are labelled; iteration prompt states "A's X + B's Y".
- check: Each option differs from the base in a named element; the final cites which options it merged.
- mistake: not stated.
- example: "I'm gonna mix A and B together"; a vampire-hunter full-mask last bid.

### R11-F23 Darker, but keep the appeal

### R11-F24 Finish only as far as useful

- mistake: Spending time polishing concept art.

### R11-F25 Keep mass away from joints
- principle: Anything that sits close to a joint can interfere technically: long hair with mass at the nape, collars near the neck. Bundle hair into one unit (Ahri's condensed braid) and keep collars away from the neck.
- why: Dramatic head motion collides with or deforms nearby costume.
- apply: Modeler leaves clearance around neck, shoulders, wrists; hair as one rigid or single-chain piece; ponytail set away from the neck.
- check: Rig test clip with extreme head turn and arm raise; no intersection at neck/collar (frame captures).
- mistake: Long hair "right against the nape".
- example: Katarina's hair moved to a ponytail away from the neck.

### R11-F26 Push and pull focal zones
- principle: Choose the focal areas (shoulders, hands), give them contrast (bright silver next to dark jacket and hair) and interesting shapes, and simplify the space between (the arm length). Do not overwhelm even the focal zone.
- why: The eye goes to the strongest contrast; quiet areas make focal ones pop.
- apply: Brief names 1-2 focal zones; the rest gets fewer details and lower contrast. Same rule for UI screens and environments.
- check: Grayscale capture: the highest value contrast sits on the named focal zones; look_check.py value map.
- mistake: Detail spread evenly along the arm.
- example: Silver shoulder spikes as "really nice bright focal point" with simplified arms.
- roblox_fit: Same.

### R11-F29 Test colours where parts overlap
- principle: The overlap of limbs is where most risk and noise live. If the palette overlaps itself (red gloves in front of red hair), the character is nearly impossible to see in motion. Fixes: darken one, two-tone the glove, recolour another part, or drop the idea; mind colour associations (red + green "immediately looks like Christmas").
- why: Same-hue parts merge when they overlap.
- apply: Review with an overlapping pose (hands in front of hair/body); colour-block so overlapping parts differ in value or hue.
- check: Capture an overlap pose at game camera; grayscale; overlapping parts must keep a value edge.
- mistake: Picking a cool colour accent without testing overlap.
- example: Red gloves vs red hair; dull camo green pants considered.
- roblox_fit: Same; also same-colour VFX over a character [inference].
