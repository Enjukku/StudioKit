Studio Flow Redesign

This page shows how the agents will work together. You give the lead your decisions. The right leads agree on the idea and the look. The crafts agree one shared plan, build in parallel and check each other. Work goes up through reviews in a fixed order. You decide at three gates.

Steps 1-3 design: approved by you Step 4 rules, skill, context: rewritten Step 5 seven job workflows: built and tested Step 6 role files: updated Step 7 gate: PASS, committed, not pushed Next: your quality audit and pilot Usage: about 5.3 M tokens

What you asked for, and how the design gives it

I give my decisions and what the game needs to the orchestrator.

The lead is your only contact. It writes your words into the job card word for word. It sizes the job, picks the job type's recipe and names one job lead.

The right leads agree on a job's idea and look before the crafts start.

Two to four leads write separate proposals. They read each other's once and name only real conflicts. The job lead writes one direction sheet. The others sign it. You pick at gate 1.

The crafts cooperate, because no one can do their part alone.

Each job has one shared plan file: a beat list, a rig sheet, a route graph, a screen state map or a feature contract. Crafts write what they need, check each other, and the owner of each question merges the result. Two rounds at most, then a small test.

Work goes back up through reviews before it reaches me, and I decide at a few clear gates.

Reviews run in a fixed order on one frozen version: scripts, the maker, the next craft, the domain lead, then QA beside the creative leads, then the lead. Reviewers never edit. Makers fix. Three gates: direction, proof, final.

Every agent follows its workflow book.

Every role book gets the same ten parts: what great work looks like, numbered steps, reference images first, style books, Roblox docs rows, self-review and its place in this chain. The Riot lessons are sorted per role.

Quality matters more than token cost; cheaper models for mechanical work.

Messages between agents stay complete. Opus runs leads, crafts and all code. Scripts run the checks that a script can do. Haiku does search, copy and format. A cheap model never approves creative quality.

What Claude Code can do on your version

I checked the papers' claims against the current Claude Code docs. I also tested messaging live, first on 2.1.296 and again on 2.1.306 after your update.

  • Subagents can message each other, but only by ID. They cannot see a list of other agents. So the lead keeps a crew list with every agent's ID and puts the IDs in the briefs.
  • On 2.1.306 a message reaches a working agent at its next step. On 2.1.296 it arrived only after the agent finished. So a change to the shared plan can now reach a craft while it works, and a fix can go back to the agent that made the part.
  • Saved workflows run steps in a fixed order, in parallel where possible. They cannot stop and wait for you. So each job type has one workflow that runs one stage per call. Your gate sits between calls.
  • Each workflow step can run a real role, such as film-director or modeler, with its own model and effort.
  • Nesting stops at 3 levels. That limits how deep agents start other agents. It does not limit how many stages a job has. The old "3 hops" rule mixed the two.
  • Agent teams stay off. They are experimental, and messaging works without them.
  • Your update to 2.1.306 brought two needed fixes: workflow resume, and a resumed agent keeping its last work. Behaviour changed between versions, so the kit re-runs these tests after every update.

Trade-offs in plain words

  1. More agent work per big job. The direction stage and the exchange rounds add calls. One study saw about 6 times more turns in mixed teams than in one agent. The gain should be less late rework. Nobody has measured this for our studio yet. Your pilot will.
  2. Three waits for you on big jobs. Medium jobs have two gates. Small jobs have none before the result.
  3. Files instead of live chat between crafts. It is slower than talk. It keeps a record, and it stops one agent's wrong answer from spreading. In one study, messages from other agents turned 11 % of correct answers wrong.
  4. One Studio writer at a time. Studio work waits in line. In return, agents cannot overwrite each other's scene.
  5. Lead reviews before you see work. This adds a step. The leads filter, they never approve. You still judge taste, because agents share blind spots.
  6. Reviewers do not edit. A review chain in one study broke 36 correct answers while it fixed 6. So makers fix, and a list of what already passed must stay passed.
  7. The animator role does not exist yet. Cutscenes and characters use a stand-in with the animator brief until job J006 builds it.

Context: what each agent knows, and how the studio learns

Results depend on context. The studio keeps knowledge in four places, each with one writer. Every agent loads context in the same order. Every job feeds the game's knowledge store (the vault), and every week the system learns from what worked and what failed.

1. How each agent finds its context

Every agent loads context in the same order. Rules and its role's skills load by themselves. The brief names the files it needs and your words. A context packet built for its role and its job adds the job files, the game notes for its craft, its Roblox docs rows and past lessons. It opens only what these name. Some context is held back on purpose: leads do not see each other's first ideas, and reviewers see the work before the maker's story.

2. How context passes between agents

Files carry the content. Messages carry pointers and news. Every brief names its inputs with their version, what must stay as it is, and who reads the output. Every hand-back names the versions it built on, so an old input shows up as stale. Your words travel word for word in the job card. Nobody passes on a summary of a summary.

3. How the vault grows with the game

At the end of every job the lead files it into the vault. Decisions go in with a reason to revisit them. Accepted work goes into a precedents list: "this model, this sound, this shot is our game". New names, systems, rejected ideas, your corrections and new reusable assets go in too. Then the integrate pass turns them into short "for this game" notes per skill. A new agent joins the project by reading what earlier jobs filed.

4. How wins and failures improve the system

Every job records its checks, its defects with their causes, its wins and its numbers. The weekly improve pass sends each cause to the layer that failed. A win that repeats becomes a recipe step or an example in a craft's book. A change stays only if its numbers hold. Otherwise it is reverted and recorded as tried and failed.

Problem found today

In this session my own skill list showed 24 of the kit's 48 skills by name only. Claude Code dropped their descriptions because the list is over its budget, least-used skills first. So the lead cannot see when to use those skills. The fix is shorter skill descriptions, with routing through roles and the context packet.

Two hooks would make routing automatic. One adds the job pointer to every agent when it starts, matched to its role. The other gives the lead its job state back after a long chat is compacted. The kit can ship both scripts. You turn them on in settings, because agents never change settings.
