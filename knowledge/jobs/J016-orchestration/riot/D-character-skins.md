## 1. Speaker, role, team, what the episode covers

**CA (League character art).**
- Annie Kwon, character artist (3D) at Riot, League of Legends skins team, ex-intern (CA:18-20). The team is PIE, "Personalization, Immersion and Expression" (CA:24-26); her sub-team is called Team Rocket (CA:38).

**Combined.** One talk is the 3D executor of someone else's concept (CA); the other is the concept author who must hand a base idea to 3D, animation and VFX (VS). Together they show both ends of the concept → 3D hand-off that our art-director → modeler chain copies.

## 2. What great work looks like (checkable signs)

**CA.**
| Sign | Check | Cite |
| --- | --- | --- |
| First read at game size | Zoomed out / squinting, it reads as a human, the coat reads as a coat; it reads as *this* champion | CA:198-208 |
| 3D form in a still image | Form shadows (AO) and highlights make convex and concave parts clear, even overstated | CA:178-186, CA:214-216 |
| Game lighting | League lights from above: up-facing surfaces lit, down-facing dark; top of hat brightest; gold nearer the camera brighter; arms read as lit cylinders | CA:96-98, CA:1010-1022, CA:1030-1032 |
| Attention hierarchy | Head, face, hair, headwear, shoulders get the eye; below the waist is darker and less saturated; boots pushed back | CA:740-744, CA:952-956, CA:1058-1062 |

| Faithful to concept | Concept executed as close to 1:1 as game limits allow; intent captured, not traced | CA:82-86, CA:850-854 |

## 3. The steps they actually follow (in their order)

**CA (3D character from a concept).**
1. Receive the concept; make it a proportional in-game character (CA:76-78).
2. High-poly sculpt in ZBrush for organic forms; she avoids box-modelling the body first because it can distort the human (CA:148-156).
3. Retopology to a game low-poly in Maya, at most 15K triangles, loops placed for the rig and animators (CA:158-168).
4. Check the new mesh against the rig in use: import the previous skin or base mesh and overlay to see joints and fingers line up (CA:864-874).
5. UV unwrap (mirrored where symmetric) (CA:170-174, CA:930-934).
6. Texture in 3D Coat: (a) mask each material's colour region first (red, blue, white, gold) (CA:70-76); (b) flat local colour (CA:80); (c) soft AO / form-shadow pass, whole body at an even pace (CA:178, CA:218-226); (d) eyes in early so it feels like a person (CA:286-290); (e) face blocked in, then left to come back later (CA:360-366, CA:402-404); (f) small-brush creases and hair strands (CA:500-514); (g) contact shadows (CA:532-540); (h) commit to harder values (CA:712-718); (i) hue-shifted colour (CA:746-788); (j) highlights, with lower parts pushed back (CA:948-956); (k) render the top half, check from the top-down game view (CA:956-962, CA:994-1000).
7. Concept-artist paintover and questions on intent at any stage; she asked for one on the high-poly sculpt (CA:790-832).
8. Test in engine, running around the map (Riot tool) (CA:1050-1058).
9. Next pass: refine textures until materials read and work together; repeat (CA:1088-1092).
10. Hand to tech art (rigging), then animation (CA:276-282).

**VS (weapon skin concept).** He names four steps: research and references, initial sketches, cleanup ("certifying"), final render (VS:41-42). In practice:
1. Brief: usually a couple of words; brands like Champions come with guidelines such as fixed colours (VS:50-51).
2. Generate many directions; shift the theme in time (ancient vs future), reframe the object (the gun as a thunderbolt factory) (VS:52-56).
3. Plan the message; research the best visual sign (Zeus → thunderbolt; the letter Z already looks like one) (VS:59-69).
4. Study the base gun's identity markers in 3P and 1P (VS:70-81).
5. Find the main character line, then the bottom line (VS:84-91, VS:104-106).
6. Add supporting dynamic lines and repeat the motif across parts (VS:122-201).
7. Add function: buttons, grip, trigger, belt hook, battery (VS:142-148, VS:189-193, VS:237-241).
8. Design the front/silencer last; hardest, camera-distorted (VS:242-248).
9. Clean line art inside the fixed silhouette (VS:218-227, VS:261-268).
10. Material splits (rubber butt pad) and moving parts for inspect animation (VS:228-236).
11. Colour: grey volumes first, then 3 colours, balance, contrast (VS:282-347).
12. Abstract placeholder for undecided parts (energy inside the glass) (VS:348-356).
13. Quick shading with adjustment layers and masks; highlights; glow for the lightning (VS:366-388).
14. Pattern detail (VS:410-415).
15. Readability checks, zoomed out and "on the ground" (VS:357-359, VS:417-431).
16. Colour variants (chromas) last (VS:432-447).
17. With more time: animations and more options; this is option one (VS:450-454).

**Combined.** Both go big-to-small: identity and silhouette, then form/value, then colour and material, then detail; colour comes late in VS (VS:282-285, VS:437) and material masks come before detail in CA (CA:70-76). Both treat the first pass as a base, not final (CA:106, VS:350).

## 4. References and research: when, what for

**CA.**
- The concept is the main reference, checked often for intent, not copied by flicking back and forth (CA:80-86).
- The game map is kept behind the model the whole time to judge colour and stand-out (CA:234-238).
- Splash and concept art for how to render hair; comic art (Mike Mignola) for where shadows and highlights go (CA:516-530).
- Painting videos teach material behaviour (gold bounce) that texturing tutorials miss (CA:670-674).
- Real objects: photographed a family member in a red blanket to learn cloth (CA:674-688); a year of life drawing for anatomy and silhouette (CA:690-696).
- Existing in-game characters and the base skin for likeness; she cooled the face because the base skin is cooler (CA:974-984).
- Rig research before modelling: which parts move, teeth, lips (CA:888-900).
- Asks the concept artist for material references (silky vs velvety) (CA:818-822).

**VS.**
- Research comes first: many references, to find the best visual explanation of the idea (VS:41, VS:65).
- Research targets the sign, not the look: the name's letters, the myth's weapon (VS:62-69).
- Outside design fields as reference for method: automotive character lines, Kia and Bugatti motif repetition (VS:84-101, VS:153-162).
- The base guns themselves are reference for what must not change (VS:70-81).
- Cultural sources for story layers (Korean tiger drawing lines in a melee) (VS:31-34).

**Combined.** References serve three jobs: what the concept intends (CA:80-86), how the game world looks and lights (CA:234-238, VS:70-81), and how materials and forms really behave (CA:674-688, VS:142-148). Research happens before drawing in VS (VS:41) and before modelling deforming parts in CA (CA:888-900).

## 5. Self-review before showing anyone

**CA.**
- Work and check from far away; squint test for the first read (CA:198-208, CA:1034).
- Check against the game map behind the model (CA:234-238).
- Rotate to the real top-down game camera; check the value structure from there (CA:994-1000).
- Run it in engine on the map (CA:1050-1058).
- Overlay on the previous skin / base mesh to confirm joints, fingers and rig fit (CA:870-874).
- "Done" check: all materials clear, reads at game height, face matches other League characters, no part looks unfinished when zoomed (CA:436-448).
- Ask "what is the best way to paint this so it reads at game height" under the time box (CA:476-480).

**VS.**
- Zoom out often; high zoom makes him over-detail (VS:357-359).
- Check first-person distortion of front details (VS:244-248) and identity in side view (VS:73-76).
- Ground-pickup test: can a player tell Phantom from Guardian fast, with part of the gun hidden (VS:417-431).
- Grey first, so colour does not hide volume problems (VS:284-285).
- Balance check of dark vs bright weight (VS:308-316).
- Toggle colour variants to prove the shape is the same and only colour volume changed (VS:440-445).

**Combined.** Both review at the game's real camera and size (CA:994-1000, VS:244-248), against the game itself (CA:234-238, CA:1050-1058, VS:417-431), and from a distance (CA:198-208, VS:357-359). CA adds a rig-fit overlay (CA:870-874); VS adds a grey-value pass (VS:284-285).

## 6. Collaboration (receive, hand on, agree)

**CA.**
| From / to | What | Cite |
| --- | --- | --- |

| 3D → concept artist | Questions on intent: is the gold vest gold armour or shiny cloth; silky or velvety; "does this feel like Caitlyn" | CA:270-276, CA:818-826 |
| Concept artist → 3D | Paintovers: drawn over a screenshot, often a before/after GIF (longer legs, face change); given on the high-poly sculpt too | CA:790-802, CA:826-832 |
| Concept artist's context | The concept artist had worked on earlier Caitlyn skins and knew what she "is supposed to be" | CA:830-832 |
| 3D → tech art (rigging) → animation | Model goes to the tech artist, who adds bones in Maya, then to animators | CA:276-282 |
| Must be agreed with tech art | New mesh matches the existing rig and base mesh, or animations do not transfer; which parts move (mouth, teeth, lips) | CA:864-878, CA:896-900 |
| Concept must anticipate rig cost | Coattails and long sleeves need extra cloth rigging; fixes are shortening or removing them | CA:908-928 |
| Closest partners | Concept art and tech art | CA:282-284 |
| Attitude | Ask each craft what it needs and meet it; do not insist on your own craft's view | CA:258-268 |
| Whole-skin cohesion | VFX and SFX take a lot of work and communication; best games have every discipline on the same vision | CA:1072-1080 |

**VS.**
| From / to | What | Cite |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Brief → concept | A couple of words; big brands add guidelines (fixed colours) | VS:50-51 |
| Concept → 3D, animation, VFX | Concept artist thinks ahead for 3D, animators and effects, and leaves room for their creativity; he makes the base idea, a "canvas", not the rules | VS:35-39 |
| Concept → animation | Designs moving parts so inspect animations have more than hand motion | VS:233-236 |
| Concept → animation / VFX | Draws mechanisms (Prime knife opening, Prelude to Chaos sword extending), sometimes with VFX sketches, to show the feeling, not to do their job | VS:360-364 |
| Concept → later decisions | Leaves an abstract placeholder (energy inside the glass) to define later | VS:348-353 |

**Combined.** The concept author owes downstream crafts: intent answers (CA:270-276), extra views when possible (CA:834-840), mechanism sketches (VS:360-364) and early warning of rig-costly shapes (CA:914-918). The 3D artist owes the rigger a mesh that fits the existing rig and moves where it must (CA:864-900). Both say each craft keeps its own craft decisions (CA:262-268, VS:37-39).

## 7. Review and approval

**CA.**
- Concept-artist paintovers and Q&A are the main craft feedback during the build (CA:790-832).
- Senior feedback on her first full-time skin: "doesn't look quite ready"; the time box then forced her to let go (CA:456-468).
- Intern feedback, repeated: Soraka's skirt grabbed attention from face, shoulders and hair (CA:1062-1066).
- Tech-art feedback comes back when the mesh breaks rig or animation (mouth sewn shut, adjust joints) (CA:874-878, CA:884-888).
- Mentoring by the skins character art lead pushed her outside her comfort zone (CA:584-594).
- Not stated: formal gate names, who signs off, number of rounds.

**VS.**
- Concepts are pitched to directors; presentation quality matters (VS:277-280).
- Art directors judge silhouette and design; colour variants alone do not count as a new design for them (VS:432-434).
- First concept is "option number one"; normal days add more options and animations (VS:452-454).
- Not stated: number of rounds, who approves the final.

**Combined.** Feedback is visual and from the author of the intent (paintover, CA:790-802) or from the next craft in the pipe (tech art, CA:874-878). Directors review ideas and silhouettes, not colour swaps (VS:432-434). Neither talk gives round counts.

## 8. Order of work: first, parallel, locked, going back

**CA.**
- Pipeline order: concept → 3D character → tech art (rig) → animation (CA:248-250, CA:276-282).
- Rig is effectively locked before 3D: a new skin must fit the existing rig (CA:864-878).
- Inside texturing: whole character at an even pace, not one part to finish (CA:220-232); soft first, harder values later (CA:712-718); hard likeness skipped and returned to (CA:984-994).
- Going back upstream: the mouth had to be reverse-engineered open after rigging found it sewn shut (CA:884-892); coattails may be cut down for rigging (CA:926-928).
- Time box ends the work, which also stops over-rendering (CA:466-490).

**VS.**
- Research → sketches → cleanup → render (VS:41-42).
- Major parts first: big/medium/small, then balance and proportions, then usability and contrast (VS:323-327).
- Major silhouette of the base gun is fixed; he works inside it with lines (VS:222-223).
- Colour and chromas last (VS:432-439).
- Undecided parts left abstract for later (VS:348-353).
- First day is slowest (one sketch); next day six, once the theme is "warm" (VS:202-207, VS:454).
- Parallel work: not described.

**Combined.** Locked first: the base (rig in CA:864-868; gun silhouette and identity in VS:73-81, VS:222-223). Late: colour and polish (VS:437, CA:1088-1092). Upstream changes happen when a downstream need appears late (CA:884-892) and are cheaper when the concept planned for it (CA:914-918).

## 9. Mistakes they name and how they avoid them

**CA.**
| Mistake | Avoidance | Cite |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Working zoomed in on face or details; far read lost | Stay at a far view; check the first read | CA:198-208 |
| Rendering only the face; the rest looks incomplete | Match pace across the whole character | CA:220-226 |
| Imposing her own cute face style | Serve the game's style and the character | CA:390-398 |
| Forgetting contact shadows | Add shadows where objects touch | CA:536-540 |
| Spelling out every hair strand | Thick-to-thin strokes that show only the shadow | CA:508-514, CA:548-558 |
| Muddy gold from greys | Saturate gold where surfaces face each other | CA:650-668 |
| Darkening a colour toward black | Hue-shift the shadow (red to purple) | CA:766-778 |
| Mouth modelled closed; rig could not open it | Research the rig and moving parts before modelling | CA:878-900 |
| Lower body too eye-catching | Darken and desaturate below the waist | CA:1062-1070 |
| Over-rendering; not knowing when to stop | Defined stopping point plus time box | CA:428-448, CA:466-476 |
| Student work was a sculpture, not a game character | Build to in-game constraints from the start | CA:646-648, CA:56-62 |
| Fear of mistakes | Loose early passes; fail fast | CA:100-106, CA:130-132 |

**VS.**
| Mistake | Avoidance | Cite |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Over-complicating to show skill | Simple, clean, readable; skip small details | VS:269-274 |
| Long, hard projects cause burnout | Keep the approach quick and simple | VS:275-276 |
| One object far too contrasty → clutter | Space contrast like letter spacing | VS:328-337 |
| Detailing at high zoom distorts the design | Return to far zoom often | VS:357-359 |
| Detail on the camera-distorted front | Few, simple shapes there | VS:244-248 |
| Endless colour debate | Colour is subjective; lean on silhouette | VS:435-439 |
| Copying the motif 1:1 | Adapt it with weight to fit the part | VS:201 |

**Combined.** Shared top mistake: too much detail too close, too early (CA:198-208, VS:269-273, VS:357-359). CA's costly one is a downstream break found late (CA:878-900).

## 10. Lessons for our AI agents
