# Place reading: guidance, scale, dressing, terrain, cohesion

## 1. The route is a contract
| ID | Practice | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| PR-01 | **Keep the game designer's route.** Collision edges, cover, routes, openings, sight lines and encounter intent are theirs. Any change that touches route length, cover, sight lines or timing is proposed to the game designer in the open, never hidden in an art pass. Check: a collision and route diff of the dressed place against the approved greybox (RD9). | RU01 O; K01-d O (greybox practice, K01-a E); J018 |
| PR-02 | **A beautiful reference image is not a route.** Turn each key-place image into an annotated target: focal point, camera, walkable area, silhouette layers, material roles, light sources, ambient life, and every unclear point. Where the image fights gameplay, ask for a ruling. | CO02 O; DN01 O |
| PR-03 | **Keep the accepted blockout.** In Blender keep a locked reference collection of it; in Studio keep blockout or validation proxies named and apart from the final art. Approve the art pass by comparing shots from the same camera, pose and route state before and after. Art snaps to the blockout's footprints (art bounding boxes within one snap step of the blockout parts). | K01-e O; BW25 O; J018 |
| PR-33 | **A constraint is dressed as the world.** A boundary, choke point or height gate becomes the most believable answer of 2-3 on theme (a cliff over the sea, a collapsed bridge, a market wall), never an invisible or plain wall; collision matches what players see (`read-plan.md` §4). Check: every boundary has a one-line "why is this here?" and its collision view matches the visible shape (RD7). | J018 |
| PR-34 | **Hazards explain themselves.** Each hazard shows where it comes from (a vent, a cracked pipe, a lava spring) and what contains it (rails, a fence, a dark crust rim); the surfaces next to it stay plain so the hazard edge reads. Its look follows the game designer's hazard sheet; the drawn danger edge never overstates or understates the real one. Check: one capture per hazard with its source in view; the edge sits on the hazard volume (RD8). | J018 |

## 2. Guiding the player
| ID | Practice | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| PR-04 | **Decide what the player must notice at each decision point before any decoration.** For each node of the route graph write a cue sentence: approach, intended next move, and why it reads (landmark, framed opening, light contrast, material change, sound source, or a blend). Then check the real camera backs the sentence. Never write "clear" without naming the object, the camera and the task. | RU02 O; G01 O; G03 O |
| PR-05 | **Far landmark and near cue are different jobs.** A far tower orients; a near doorway gives the next move. A tower seen through every wall may stop reading as a route; a doorway that looks like facade gets missed. Review how the two work together. Global orientation, district identity and local callouts are three separate jobs. | G02 O; K05-b O |
| PR-06 | **Design a far landmark twice:** as a place you can enter and as a shape seen from its approach. Literal scale can fail: small things at true scale read as specks from afar, so stylized massing, spacing or framing may serve the far view, while traversal sizes stay as approved. | K04-a E (Nintendo); K04-b O |
| PR-07 | **A destination can pull before the path is clear.** Pair a long-term landmark with short local cues; reveal enough to keep curiosity going; keep negative space so the focal object relates clearly to its surroundings. Never present an unreachable landmark as explorable if the game says "what you see you can reach". | K08-b O; K08-c O |
| PR-08 | **Three views per decision:** the approach (is the cue found in time), the decision point (is the choice clear), and after committing (is the next goal readable). Add the return trip if backtracking is allowed. | G05 O |
| PR-09 | **Enclosure is not safety.** "Sheltered from these sightlines" and "safe under these game rules" are different statements. Get the game state from the game designer before trying to convey calm, danger or contest. | G12 O; G10-G11 E |
| PR-10 | **The environment supports the characters; it does not compete.** Keep simple backgrounds under repeated decisions and rich detail where the player stops to look. Judge the place with characters in typical avatar colours (players pick any colours), a UI state, an ability effect and an interactive object in view: a background rich on its own can bury an attack. Check: busiest-moment capture, character subject/ring ratio (RD3, VB2). | K02-a E; K02-d O; CO03 O; J018 |
| PR-35 | **The first view tells where you are and what to do.** Spawn position and facing (the game designer's) frame the goal or main landmark as the strongest focal point of the first frame; the 3-5 key actions of the space each have a visible cue. Never spawn facing a wall. Check: first frame after spawn at the default camera (RD2). | J018 |
| PR-36 | **Markers on the approach, borders on the floor.** Objective markers sit where players approach (a worn sign, a big symbol on the landmark facing the incoming side), never only inside the objective where they are redundant; the objective area is separated in the floor by a border trim inside the environment palette, so it says "here" without UI. Check: from each spawn route a marker is visible before entry; the floor border reads in a phone-size view (RD5). | J018 |
| PR-37 | **The landmark is the tallest, clearest shape,** with a silhouette unique among the stage's landmarks (not only a unique colour), visible from every route fork and from most of its zone; each zone or team base has an identity symbol (a statue, a banner shape) visible from its entry. Check: landmark height above its neighbours from part sizes; one capture from each fork (RD13). | J018 |
| PR-38 | **Shape flow and light point at the goal.** Paths, walls, roof lines, cliff edges, debris and the light gradient lead to the focal point or the next route; slopes and tilted shapes lean toward it; remove lines that lead into dead ends or off-screen. A few small accents (a bird, a flag, a coloured lantern) may sit along that eye path, always smaller and lower in contrast than the focal point. Long sight-line walls may carry a trim or ledge at character eye height (measured in Studio) where the game designer asks for it; none where a prop already shows player height. Check: lines traced on the grey first view (RD13). | J018 |
| PR-39 | **A surprise hides outside the light.** A bright focal light pulls attention so strongly that the darker side goes unwatched: stage an ambush or a hidden reward in the low-contrast area or behind a turn or rise, and show the next big thing framed as the player comes round. Check: in the grey capture the surprise sits outside the top focus (RD12). | J018 |
| PR-40 | **Something to see within each walk; views at the pauses.** The game designer's route table gives each segment's seconds at walk speed; I make sure each segment shows something (a landmark, a story cluster, a new view) within the table's limit, and that vistas and new views open at natural pauses and real achievements, not at random points. Check: per segment, what the player sees (RD10). | J018 |
| PR-41 | **Every piece says what it is and what you can do here.** A cliff reads as a cliff and as climbable; every climbable ledge, shootable panel or breakable crate uses the same treatment (shape, value or material) everywhere in the game; a place that hides what is interactive fails, and so does a readable place that looks like a test level. Check: the treatment table against the place; the game designer names what each place is and what can be done there at the check-in (RD6). | J018 |

## 3. Scale
| ID | Practice | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| PR-11 | **One scale convention everywhere.** Roblox's standard is 1 stud = 28 cm; the engine does no unit conversion, so the project must keep one convention across Blender, Studio, gameplay and audio. It is a conversion, not an avatar height or an order to build photoreal proportions. | RU04 V+O; SC01 V (fact-check-performance-import) |
| PR-12 | **Camera, collision envelope and movement are inputs from day one.** The scale sheet holds: the real avatar envelope, camera mode and offsets, supported avatar scaling, walk speed and abilities (jump, climb, mount, tool swing), interaction reach, held items that change clearance. Take them from the game designer and runtime config; measure the jump in Studio (the default character uses jump power, so the reachable height is a measurement, not a remembered number). | RU03 O; SC02 O; J018 |
| PR-13 | **Three clearances, three fixes:** visual (does it look like it fits), collision (does the real collision shape pass), camera (does the third-person camera stay usable on the way through). An ornate arch can look wide while its collision blocks; an opening can pass collision while the camera clips the lintel. | SC03 O |
| PR-14 | **No universal door or path size.** Derive each opening from the motion it must allow, that motion's collision envelope and a design margin. Paths add passing traffic, combat spacing and whether they should feel tight; ceilings add jumps and overhead gear. Roblox's own greybox sample keeps doorways and halls at least 10 studs wide and walls at least 10 studs tall for two passing players and the camera (`tutorials/curriculums/environmental-art/greybox-your-environment.md:77`): an example, not a preset. | SC04 O; DN02 O; J018 |
| PR-15 | **Exaggerate on purpose.** Bigger-than-real scale for shelter, grandeur or threat is allowed when intended. Record which relations bend (roof height, rock size, door framing, distance squeeze); gameplay openings stay tied to the route contract. | SC06 O |
| PR-16 | **Scale review:** the same spot with the player character, a familiar prop and the real camera; then once without the familiar prop to see if the place's own proportions carry the scale. | SC07 O |
| PR-42 | **A player-sized reference in every space, a known-size object beside every big one.** A player dummy stands in every greybox space and every review scene; interaction props (doors, stairs, rails, seats, counters) are sized to the character first; doors, plants, railings or crates sit near large focal structures so they read as grand, not as "maybe five buildings tall". Check: sizes in studs against the character; one known-size object near each focal structure (FL7). | J018 |

## 4. Set dressing
| ID | Practice | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| PR-17 | **Dressing is evidence, not a quota.** Before placing a cluster, write who used it, why it is still there, what state the place is in, and whether it falsely promises loot or interaction. Decide the reading (delivery, storage, barricade, abandonment) first, then the spread. | K06-b O; SD01 O |
| PR-18 | **Composition-critical vs swappable.** An arch that frames the goal is part of the composition contract; a rope on the floor can change. Tag each object's role in the scene manifest so a cleanup pass knows what needs review. | SD02 O |
| PR-19 | **Calm space is designed.** Walkable ground and the player band (floor to head height) keep a quiet value and saturation structure so characters, pickups and effects read; detail clusters sit above head height, at edges and in alcoves where the player stops. "Empty" is about function, not instance count. Check: detail spots in a phone-size view of each key view (FL2). | SD03 O; J018 |
| PR-20 | **Repetition is composition.** Vary grouping, rotation, spacing, height, material treatment and surroundings before asking for more unique meshes; no kit piece seen twice at the same angle in one view. Judge a repeated facade from the camera path players use (a pan shows a repeating highlight or window beat that a still hides). Check: key views (FL3). | SD04 O; SD06 O; RU11 O; J018 |
| PR-21 | **Intersection can be construction.** A rock sunk into a bank is fine; a chair hovering over the floor is a defect. Record where overlap is approved, the ground contact expected and the clearance moving parts need. For each prop family record: support surface, pivot, slope handling, collision role, visibility priority, placement exclusions (route lanes, interaction approaches, camera corridors, sound anchors, VFX attach zones). Keep walk lanes clear of props instead of switching off collision on props players can reach. | SD07 O; SD08 O |
| PR-22 | **Detail where attention is.** A first-person shooter put detail above head height; a third-person phone camera sees a different band. Measure the band your camera really shows, and budget detail by display size and speed: areas players run past get big forms only; stop points and landmarks get more; props near paths are finished on all sides because the camera orbits. | K01-c E; K01-f O; J018 |
| PR-43 | **Someone lives here; time has passed.** Write 3 story facts per stage (who lives or works here, what happened, climate and age) and 1-2 history facts per hero prop; give each fact visible carriers (wear where hands and feet go, repairs, leaves under trees, sand drifted against walls, chipped stone, laundry, food left on a table), big enough for the game camera on a phone and at backdrop contrast. A spotless, "first person ever here" place reads dead. Check: every main area has at least one story cluster carrying an assigned fact; the art director names a fact at the check-in (FL5). | J018 |
| PR-44 | **Floors stay clean for the smallest gameplay object.** In play areas ground cover is added, then cut back to a whisper; decorative material on a floor stays small enough not to read as a different floor (and a different footstep). Check: the smallest gameplay object on each floor type at phone size (FL1). | J018 |
| PR-45 | **Ground every object.** Hero props get a base patch, plinth or skirt one value step from the open ground; rock and prop bases blend into the ground (painted transition, sand, small debris) instead of a straight clean seam; a thin dark contact shadow where things touch; a soft hint of the object's hue on the ground near it; hazards get a darker rim and plain neighbours. Check: audit 0 floating; contact close-ups (FL6). | J018 |
| PR-46 | **Size steps in every group.** Rocks, crystals, plants and debris come as one big, one medium, one small, never neighbours of equal size; few pieces with clear steps beat many equal ones. Check: bounding sizes of siblings in a cluster (FL4). | J018 |
| PR-47 | **Backdrop pieces: simple surfaces, elegant gesture.** Plants and backdrop props stay low in triangles and texture but have a clear, flowing curve that reads from afar and in peripheral vision; a few upright grass cards at edges and rest areas give flat ground depth, never on play floors. | J018 |
| PR-51 | **Secrets off the path.** Small hidden secrets that reward discovery (a recurring mascot, a symbol, an event teaser) sit off routes and decision points and never weaken the read; which ones and how many is the game designer's call. Check: RD12. | J018 |

## 5. Terrain and nature
| ID | Practice | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| PR-23 | **Terrain or mesh, by what the shape needs.** Terrain for continuous sculptable volume and smooth material blends; meshes for chosen silhouettes, hard stylized strata, overhangs, modular reuse or exact UV direction. Combine big terrain masses with rock and foliage kits; check seams from eye height and from the ends of traversal. No source proves one is always cheaper: compare on the target phone. | TN03 O; TNT4 O |
| PR-24 | **A biome is a coordinated set:** palette, material, wind and sound together, with a narrow vocabulary and a written exception list (dominant ground cover, drainage, signs of moisture, typical sound sources, human traces, how it meets its neighbors). A swamp is not "green plants plus fog". Restrained variety with strong colour makes regions memorable. | TN04 O; K03-a E; K03-c O |
| PR-25 | **Water is several jobs:** the visible surface, how the player moves in it, its sound sources, mist, and the shoreline dressing. | TN04 O |
| PR-26 | **Scatter repeatably.** Use masks and recorded seeds so a path change regenerates its margins; keep exclusion zones around routes, doors, landmarks and interaction surfaces. A new seed is a new scene, not a neutral rebuild. | K03-d O; H07 O |
| PR-27 | **Protect an organic landmark's silhouette at every viewing distance before adding cracks and detail.** | TN04 O |
| PR-48 | **Ground material follows a rule.** Assign terrain and part materials by height and slope (rock on steep faces, grass on flats, dirt at transitions) with one script and record it, so a change regenerates cleanly. Play spaces stay mostly flat; every height change in a play space is on the game designer's intentional list, and no surprise height hides a threat from the camera. | J018 |

## 6. Cohesion with the art director
| ID | Practice | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| PR-28 | **The art bible is a versioned input.** Before a stage, note its version, palette roles, value bands, reserved colours, shape families, material response, light direction, detail hierarchy, anchors, and what stays flexible. Naming the flexible parts keeps places from becoming clones. | CO01 O; J018 |
| PR-29 | **One target slice before multiplying detail.** Pick the hardest representative section (art, movement, light, ambience, performance together) and prove the style there at the lowest and highest graphics setting, with characters, UI and gameplay effects present. The art director reviews; the owner decides direction changes. | RU05 O; W04 O; ST05 O; J018 |
| PR-30 | **One world premise, shown everywhere.** Invent the place's cause-and-effect premise, then check terrain, architecture, props, VFX and ambience all show matching consequences. A landmark's build state can tell history with no sign. | K10-b O; K09-b O |
| PR-31 | **Respect real cultures.** Cultural references are checked by qualified reviewers; looking recognizable does not excuse a stereotype. Never copy another game's layout, landmarks, names or assets. | K05-d O; K10-c O; level-design §5 |
| PR-32 | **Review notes name the layer.** Location and camera, the observed defect, the intended reading, the likely cause, the responsible specialist, what must be kept, and the evidence needed. No mood-only notes. A visual note is answered with a paint-over on the capture (`reference-images`) before the 3D change. | CO08 O; RV04 O; J018 |
| PR-49 | **The place has a character and a theme.** The personality words drive shape language and materials; 2-3 theme cues (crystals in the sand of a space desert, timber shoring in a mining town) appear across ground, rocks and debris so the place is never "just a desert". Check: theme cues present per area (FL8). | J018 |
| PR-50 | **One shape vocabulary, down to the small shapes, one stylisation level.** Rocks, trims, ornaments and debris use the game's shape family, with the art bible's meaning (sharper shapes where the place threatens, softer where it is safe); the detail level of the environment matches the characters' (stylised characters do not stand in a photoreal world). A strong motif found by another craft is recorded and reused in the place. | J018 |
