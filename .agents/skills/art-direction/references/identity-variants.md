# Identity: characters, props, places, variants, tiers and themes

> Author: J018 maker art-director · claude-opus-5-5 · high · 2026-10-22. Part of `art-direction`; built also from the owner's Riot transcripts (J018), with the old identity-contract rules of the J009 skill (01:LL-02, LL-06, 05:AF11-AF12); repair round 2: the anchor count by pointer. The standards for who or what a subject is and how it stays recognisable; the checks are lines of `art-review/references/self-review.md` (ids in brackets).

## 1. Know the subject before any image
| Card | Holds | Used by |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Character card | temperament, history, the two-word tension ("elegant + deadly"), how it moves, what it would never wear, 2-4 personality adjectives | every prompt, the modeler's read plan, the animator's motion profile, the sound cue card |
| Prop or place personality | 3-5 words ("rigid, old, timeless"), mapped to shape language and materials | modeler, environment designer |
| Sell list | 2-4 numbered things the image or asset must sell (faction, role, personality, the fantasy); each candidate is scored against it | every visual brief; icon and thumbnail promise |
| Role | the gap it fills in the roster (front line, ranged, pet, tower); the bible maps role to shape and colour cues | game designer states it before the brief |
| Archetype traits | for a known archetype (knight, ninja, dragon), the traits that make it work; kept in every option | options round |

- Every choice traces to a card line; a choice with no line is cut [DR8, ID7].
- Real anatomy and construction: joints and masses follow bones; creatures combine named real animal parts; every costume piece shows how it is attached. Ask the rigger and animator about mass near joints before a design is approved (`rig-pipeline` S1) [ID11].
- Hard cases are sized early: a scary semi-humanoid is easy to make silly; a multi-form character is several characters of work.
- Represent a stated ethnicity or culture accurately; move a theme that could read badly onto a prop motif instead of an act or scene (`platform-policy`) [ID10].
- Design for motion and the real views: test the design in extreme poses, tiny on screen, from above and behind, blurred in a fast frame; nothing drapes in front of the shoulders and arms [ID11, SH8].
- **Build for the rig that exists:** read the rig sheet first; concept prompts name the dangling parts the rig already moves and forbid parts with no joint. Costly parts (capes, loose cloth, long hair, new joint chains) stay only when they carry the theme and the character is not costly elsewhere; each has a written reason and a cheaper fallback the rigger confirms.

## 2. The style levers and the theme
- **Set each lever on purpose:** saturation range, how far proportions are pushed, realism of props and places, the value structure (designed groups vs photoreal noise). Characters and places share one stylisation level; pushed cartoon characters in a desaturated realistic world read as two games [DR7].
- **Every shape and colour has a job** in saying the idea; a design is done when no shape is random. A clean style has no semi-transparent overlays and little airbrush.
- **Theme fit can rule out a cool idea:** an option that reads as the wrong genre or tone is rejected even if it is strong [DR6].
- **Each theme gets a library** before character work: 3-5 shorthand motifs the audience already links to it, shape language, iconography, a name and a logo; one message and its one symbol (a name's letterforms can give the shape), repeated as the motif; costume, prop, UI and VFX draw on it [SH10].
- **Theme cues so a place is not generic:** 2-3 signature materials or cues across ground, rocks and debris; a fresh reader names the theme without UI [ID8].
- **Each theme needs its own niche:** one line saying how it differs from the nearest existing theme [DR6].
- **A named blend makes a style:** when the bible blends two sources, it names what comes from each so the style does not drift toward one.
- **Make it for a specific audience:** the brief names the target player and a one-line "fans want"; options are judged by that audience's likely excitement, not the maker's taste.

## 3. Identity anchors and likeness
- Every character and item family has **a few identity anchors** players know it by (count: the bible's §7 row; G1 shows 2-3): silhouette features, signature proportions, the primary read (the source of power: weapon, casting hand, core), signature colours. Item families get a ranked list of recognition features. The bible holds both (`art-review/references/art-bible.md` §7).
- **Silhouette first, face second:** the items a character always carries go into the silhouette; the face is pushed enough to read at the smallest crop players see (icon, shop card) [SH2].
- **Head, shoulders and hands carry the read;** the source of power keeps its size and thickness in every variant [RD13].
- **One likeness everywhere:** face, posture and anchors match in model, icon, UI portrait, cutscene and key art; the anchor images ride with every prompt; the last question before hand-back is "same person?" [ID2].
- **The fantasy survives every real view:** tiny on screen, from above or behind, in a fast frame [ID11].
- **Faces come early:** eyes and the focal feature go in at the first pass; before any review the face is at its best, or every note will be about it.

## 4. Variants, skins and updates
- **A variant keeps every anchor and the base proportions;** theme, material, colour and costume change. If a pose hides one anchor, accept it only when all others are visible [ID1].
- **A dropped landmark leaves its slot filled** with something of similar mass (a hat replaced by tall hair, never by nothing, never by a comic oversized version on a serious character) [ID1].
- **Variant or same character?** A skin changes the palette (a new version); the same character in a new scene keeps the base palette; record which it is [ID3].
- **One clarity budget** across model, motion, VFX and sound; parity both ways; a variant re-themes the abilities (its effects and sounds), not only the costume.
- **Twist within bounds:** a darker or stranger variant keeps the character's poise and appeal; the twist is one line ("keep the square head, swap the stone face for cooling lava because the hammer cracked the sun"), justified by lore.
- **Costume first, prop second;** the prop shares at least one costume motif; small theme nods sit on existing anchors as shape changes, not added clutter; inherited parts that make no sense in the new theme are replaced by a theme-fitting part of similar mass.
- **A complex signature item signals a skilled owner:** hero items keep more distinct big parts than common ones; simplifying until generic loses quality.
- **Explore costumes on varied poses and body types** (2-3 attitude poses); the stiff three-quarter turnaround comes only at the end, for the modeler. Variant concepts start from a mannequin render of the base character at the game camera as the image-edit input, so proportions stay on model.
- **Through a narrative lens:** each variant has a two-line story of the character's role in the new world.
- **Updates keep the kernel:** list what players love (kept, brought forward) and what is dated (trimmed or justified) before a remake; anchors come from the strongest canon (`platform-evolution-marketing.md` §2).

## 5. Tiers and cosmetics
- **Look follows real rank:** rarer, stronger or more valuable tiers differ by silhouette and finish in the order of the progression designer's tier table (the rank ladder and the rows allowed a top-tier break come from it); a cheap item never wears the top tier's look [ID6].
- **Standard items follow the bible;** the top tier may break the mould only by a documented exception the art director proposes and the owner decides; premium tiers split into parts that take different finishes (gloss against matte, a shimmer on one frame), and keep at least the base's secondary motion [ID6].
- **Cosmetics never change the gameplay read or cost gameplay:** the class silhouette band holds (starting value: black-fill area within about 10 % of the base, PROPOSAL; the game design table is the value's source), cosmetic effects stay below combat-effect brightness, nothing hides combat information [ID5].
- **Paid random tiers** (eggs, crates, gacha): the tier look follows the rules in `platform-policy` (`references/rules.md`); the reveal shares one windup for every result and no lower result shows a higher tier's layers. The design and VFX books own the reveal; you check that tier colours, frames and shapes follow the bible and never suggest better odds.

## 6. Small accents and story layers
- Big areas tell role and faction; 2-4 small accents (a pin, a decal, a charm) tell who the person is, sized to stay visible at the game camera [ID9].
- **Brand first, hidden story second:** the one-glance read (theme, rarity, role) comes first; a hidden story detail is small or medium, invisible at thumbnail size and findable close up [ID8].
